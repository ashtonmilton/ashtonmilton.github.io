A university updates its student records so applicants can choose from dozens of gender identities. An employer adds pronouns to email signatures. A public agency replaces a binary sex field with a third option. Each reform can remove a real indignity. None determines who will be expected to take unpaid leave when a child falls ill, which colleague will be praised for authority while another is penalised as abrasive or why an institution presumed the right to ask about gender in the first place.

There is an uncomfortable possibility here. A society can become remarkably accommodating about the names of the boxes while remaining thoroughly conservative about what the boxes do. It can expand the catalogue of recognised identities while preserving the underlying distribution of care, authority, credibility and risk.

That is the tension this essay pursues. Is emancipation the freedom to define ourselves within gender’s categories or freedom from the authority those categories exercise over our lives? The first has become a prominent language of contemporary reform. The second demands far greater political ambition. If we end up with a hundred recognised genders, each quietly trailing its own expectations regarding temperament, labour, appearance and belonging, we will have diversified the vocabulary of constraint rather than dismantled it. Yet any programme of gender abolition that demands individuals renounce the identities through which they currently navigate the world reproduces coercion in the name of liberation.

The resolution lies in separating two claims that contemporary politics routinely conflates. Self-identification is a claim individuals make upon society; institutional classification is a power claim society exercises over individuals. Those claims are asymmetrical and they cannot carry equal presumptions. Gender must be stripped of its presumed jurisdiction over every domain of life that does not require it. Individuals hold authority to name themselves; institutions hold no unilateral right to decide when those names—or categories assigned without consent—carry material consequences. Whenever an organisation invokes gender, it must bear a strict, externally enforceable burden of proof.

## The Third Box

Consider the objection that recognising non-binary identities merely creates a third category and thereby legitimises the first two. The critique begins from a genuine political instinct: why answer the rigid division of human beings by multiplying divisions?

Yet it mistakes two fundamentally different acts. A person who states that neither man nor woman describes them is refusing the exhaustiveness of the binary, not petitioning for a third uniform complete with its own prescribed duties. Robin Dembroff’s (2020) account of genderqueer identities as critical gender kinds captures this philosophical distinction: certain forms of identification gain meaning precisely by opposing the assumption that men and women exhaust human possibility. Their existence does not ratify the original categories as natural, permanent or politically desirable.

Institutions, however, can turn refusal into routine administration. In digital form, ‘non-binary’ is swiftly domesticated into another mandatory data field to be stored, cross-referenced and interpreted. Its surface inclusiveness conceals the prior, more radical question: why was this information demanded for this transaction at all? The political failure is not that someone identifies outside the binary. The failure is the institution’s unchallenged entitlement to sort human beings by gender even when it cannot articulate why gender has any bearing on the matter at hand.

This dynamic reaches far beyond bureaucratic forms. Suppose a school lets every pupil use their chosen name and pronouns while still channelling students along gendered lines in subject choice, dress codes and disciplinary expectations. Allowing students to name themselves protects them from daily humiliation and that protection is non-negotiable. Calling it emancipation, however, is an evasion. When institutions treat symbolic recognition as an alibi for leaving gendered hierarchies intact, recognition stops being a step towards freedom and becomes a technique for managing dissent.

## The Difficult Inheritance of Abolition

The ambition to make gender politically irrelevant carries a demanding intellectual lineage. Monique Wittig (1981) argued that ‘woman’, under conditions of heterosexual domination, names a political class rather than an eternal human essence. On this view, liberation requires abolishing the social and material relations that produce that class, not offering a more flattering celebration of what its members supposedly are.

Wittig’s materialism directly challenges any politics satisfied with affirmation. If a category is constituted through subordination rather than merely burdened by it after the fact, one cannot strip away the structural hierarchy and expect the subjective identity to emerge untouched as an innocent personal preference. Identities forged inside systems of asymmetrical power may shift, fracture and lose their primacy once those systems are dismantled.

Why, then, reject coercive abolitionism in the present? Not because contemporary gender identities are untouched by history or power, nor because liberation leaves them frozen in place, but because coercive erasure from above replicates the very domination it claims to overthrow. People survive, build solidarity and render themselves intelligible through the categories currently available to them; trans and gender-nonconforming people in particular have fought for recognition against state erasure and social violence. Emancipation cannot be imposed by stripping individuals of the language they use to make sense of their lives.

Abolishing gender hierarchy cannot guarantee the survival of contemporary identities in their present form, nor does it guarantee their disappearance. The political objective is to transform the relations through which gender acquires compulsory authority. What people subsequently make of gender—as identity, affiliation, expression or something no longer central to social life—must remain a question they are free to determine rather than an outcome prescribed by the state.

Katharine Jenkins (2016) clarifies the conceptual terrain by distinguishing gender-as-class—one’s structural position within systems of power—from gender-as-identity, one’s internal sense of lived selfhood. A definition of womanhood focused solely on structural subordination cannot simply declare trans women conceptually inconvenient, just as an account focused solely on identity cannot map the material distribution of unpaid care or gendered violence.

Accepting Jenkins’s distinction, however, immediately raises a harder institutional problem. If political theory requires both concepts, which one—if either—may an institution invoke when distributing resources, duties or protections? When an employer designs parental leave, should it govern by gender identity, presumed sex, the physiological fact of giving birth or actual caregiving responsibility? The answer cannot be whichever metric the organisation happens to keep on file. A conceptual distinction acquires political teeth only when it restricts who has the authority to classify—and denies institutions the licence to use personal identity as an administrative shortcut.

## The Power to Make Gender Relevant

An employer need not issue a written directive ordering women to perform the emotional and administrative housekeeping of an office. If colleagues routinely default to women for mentoring, note-taking and conflict mediation—tasks that carry high time costs and negligible prestige—and promotion depends on visible output, inequality reproduces itself without a single formal rule. Individuals remain legally free to decline, yet the reputational penalty for declining is unequally distributed.

This limitation is precisely where freedom understood merely as the absence of legal interference breaks down. A person can have formal permission to refuse an expectation yet lack any realistic power to do so without paying a steep price. Republican theories of non-domination focus on power exercised over others without adequate checks or contestation. Alan Coffee’s (2013) reading of Mary Wollstonecraft sharpens this insight: formal legal equality routinely coexists with inherited social hierarchies that distort who can speak with authority, whose grievances register as credible and what counts as a reasonable demand.

It is tempting to draw a neat boundary between hard institutional power and soft cultural norms—insulating attraction, dress, religious observance and personal demeanour from political scrutiny while reserving critique for employment and state coercion. The boundary becomes difficult to sustain when cultural expectations govern material decisions. Informal cultural norms governing femininity, deference and emotional availability operate as mechanisms of domination when social and institutional arrangements make conformity the price of credibility, livelihood or safety. While republican non-domination stops short of policing private desire or personal aesthetics through state decree, it cannot leave informal workplace and public culture untouched: dismantling asymmetric social penalties requires stripping subjective norms of institutional power—replacing opaque judgements of ‘demeanour’ or ‘authority’ with transparent criteria for pay, promotion and task allocation so cultural bias cannot quietly govern material outcomes.

I call the institutional expression of this power compulsory relevance. It has two forms: explicitly making sex or gender determine a decision whose purpose does not require that classification and allowing informal gender expectations to govern consequential judgements without justification. The mechanisms differ, but both grant gender an authority over pay, care or credibility that individuals cannot readily contest. Classification rules and decision-making practices therefore require scrutiny together.

Parental leave offers a clear illustration. Leave tied to pregnancy, childbirth and physical recovery addresses specific physiological needs. Leave to care for a child addresses a social responsibility. When a policy treats mothers as default carers and fathers as secondary assistants, it assigns a life-shaping role through gender when the pertinent fact is who is undertaking care. Renaming the policy in gender-neutral language while leaving its underlying assumptions and workplace penalties intact changes nothing. De-gendering care allocation is far more emancipatory than inventing a new administrative label for every conceivable family arrangement.

The governing principle is straightforward: any institution that makes gender consequential must demonstrate a specific, non-circular connection between the classification and the task. Tradition is not a justification. Administrative convenience cannot override personal independence. Wherever a narrower, task-specific fact can perform the same work—caregiving responsibility, clinical need, demonstrated individual risk, relevant competence—the broader gender category must yield.

Replacing a broad gender marker with detailed questions about anatomy or household life is not automatically liberating. The proposed standard is justified collection, restricted use and accountable retention. An institution should collect only information needed for a stated, defensible purpose; access and reuse should be limited accordingly. Some clinical records, equality monitoring and legal accountability require information to be retained over time. Retention should follow a documented justification and review period, not an automatic demand either to erase everything immediately or to preserve an unrestricted personal archive.

## Safety Is Where Easy Answers Fail

Hard cases test whether a political principle has genuine spine or collapses into slogan-making. Consider a domestic abuse refuge operating with communal sleeping and bathing facilities. Its residents have fled severe violence and require predictability, privacy and trauma-informed protection. A trans woman escaping an abusive partner requests emergency admission. Only one bed is available, facilities are shared and there is no spare capacity elsewhere in the network that night. Whose claim prevails?

The normative question cannot be settled by treating identity as an automatic entitlement or sex as an automatic proxy for danger. Admission must consider the applicant’s immediate exposure to abuse alongside the existing residents’ specific safeguarding needs. Neither a generalised suspicion nor a generalised assurance can substitute for that assessment.

If the existing residents’ needs can be protected through arrangements genuinely available that night, I would admit the applicant. Her gender history alone supplies no evidence that she will harm anyone. However, the hypothetical does not establish whether adequate privacy or trauma support can actually be provided. Admission cannot be pronounced harmless simply by assuming those resources into existence.

Suppose an existing resident instead has a serious, individualised need that cannot be accommodated in the shared facilities and would be substantially endangered by admission. The decision must then compare the severity, immediacy and likelihood of harm to that resident with the danger facing the applicant if refused. Neither person’s claim disappears. Current occupancy is relevant because protection is already being provided, but it cannot be treated as an absolute priority regardless of the newcomer’s danger.

The same allocation problem remains throughout: an existing resident, an applicant and one unused bed, with no alternative placement available that night. If the evidence establishes that admission would produce the more serious immediate safeguarding harm, refusal may be justified under the proposed standard; if the greater danger lies in leaving the applicant outside, admission may be justified. Where the risks cannot be reliably ranked, there is no algorithmic resolution. Staff must record a reasoned judgement, maintain contact with the person left exposed and seek whatever emergency protection is actually available. The principle constrains the reasons for the decision; it does not guarantee a safe outcome under the stipulated scarcity.

It would be dishonest to resolve a scarcity-defined problem by pretending abundance exists—by invoking an imaginary, well-funded support system that effortlessly supplies a comparable bed elsewhere. A refusal at the door may expose a survivor to further violence. When finite capacity forces that outcome, it indicts the political decision to ration protection, even when the immediate allocation is defensible. The long-term response must include sufficient, varied, safe refuge provision. Funding cannot be conjured into existence for tonight’s decision, but its absence remains a political choice with material consequences.

This argument proposes a normative standard; it is not a statement of the legal rules currently governing refuge admission. A provider’s lawful service configuration, safeguarding obligations and use of equality-law exceptions require separate legal assessment. I do not infer that every provider must adopt one configuration or that an abstract principle overrides existing duties. More varied provision, including self-contained accommodation where feasible, could reduce conflicts between privacy and access. Such provision is a policy proposal, not accommodation that can be presumed available in tonight’s case.

## The Limits of Institutional Sight

Rejecting compulsory relevance does not mean demanding institutional blindness. In healthcare, clinical safety frequently depends on knowing whether a patient can become pregnant, takes exogenous hormones or has particular reproductive organs. Relying on a legal gender marker in place of targeted physiological questions can lead to missed diagnoses or dangerous prescribing. Yet clinical necessity justifies asking the specific question relevant to the treatment at hand, under strict medical confidentiality—not compiling an exhaustive anatomical inventory as a condition of routine care.

A second objection is statistical: if institutions stop collecting gender categories, how can society detect and prove systemic discrimination? Pay disparities, occupational segregation and patterns of violence vanish from view when groups are no longer counted. Evidence of structural injustice depends on the very classifications that emancipation seeks to weaken.

The answer lies in maintaining a strict distinction between measuring patterned disadvantage and distributing opportunities by presumed identity. Equality audits and social surveys may require carefully designed categories—capturing sex, gender identity and caregiving status separately where relevant—to expose where hierarchy persists. Data collection must be proportionate to the purpose, with confidentiality and appropriate limits on linkage, disclosure and reuse. Individual participation should be voluntary wherever it is not genuinely necessary. One institutional use renders injustice visible; another can reproduce it.

I therefore propose safeguards that separate equality monitoring from decisions about individuals: purpose-specific access, restrictions on linkage and disclosure, proportionate retention and independent scrutiny. Genuine anonymisation should be used where the purpose permits it; some longitudinal or accountability work requires protected identifiable records instead. Individuals should have effective means to challenge consequential classifications. These safeguards are proposed institutional requirements, not a claim that existing law already supplies an identical remedy in every context.

## A Narrower Jurisdiction

Polite pluralism is not liberation. Adding options to a dropdown menu while leaving gendered divisions of labour, authority and credibility intact is simply a more refined administration of hierarchy—one in which women still absorb the career penalties of unpaid care, men face quiet status punishments for refusing stoicism or dominance and trans and non-binary people must still surrender intimate medical histories or negotiate institutions built around two rigid administrative tracks.

A genuinely radical politics of gender requires revoking gender’s jurisdictional authority across public and economic life. It means stripping employers, schools, landlords and state agencies of their presumed right to treat sex or gender as a proxy for competence, temperament, caregiving duty or risk. It means restructuring workplace and public evaluation so informal cultural sanctions cannot govern pay or credibility, constraining the bureaucratic appetite for intimate bodily facts and funding public infrastructure—from universal childcare to self-contained refuges—so basic security never depends on fitting an institution’s preferred category.

Who, then, decides when gender matters? Individuals should retain authority over how they name themselves. Institutions should justify consequential uses of sex or gender by reference to a legitimate purpose, proportionate information practices and decisions open to independent challenge. Emancipation is measured neither by the number of recognised identities nor by the suppression of those identities. Its measure is the authority people gain to contest gender’s unexamined power over their lives.
