<p><em>China's household registration system does more than record where people belong on paper. It shapes how families access public services in the cities where they actually live, particularly in education.</em></p>

<p><em>Hukou</em> (户口) refers to an individual's household registration within China's broader <em>huji</em> (户籍) system. Historically, registration tied access to public services, including schooling, healthcare and social protection, to a person's official locality. Successive reforms have expanded services for internal migrants, but living in a city still does not automatically confer the same administrative entitlements as holding local <em>hukou</em> (State Council, 2014, 2026). For families moving across municipal or provincial boundaries, school enrolment can consequently involve residence documentation, parental employment records and social-insurance requirements.</p>

<p>Understanding how <em>hukou</em> shapes education requires separating three questions: whether a child has a legal right to attend school, whether their family satisfies local admissions requirements, and how a particular school place is allocated.</p>

<h2>What Does Hukou Actually Record?</h2>

<p>China's household registration system, formally known as <em>huji</em> (户籍), records an individual's registered household and locality. Historically, it distinguished agricultural and non-agricultural registration. A major State Council reform in 2014 called for replacing those formal categories with a unified resident registration system, while retaining differentiated conditions for obtaining local registration in cities of different sizes (State Council, 2014).</p>

<p>An individual's registered locality can therefore differ from where they live. Someone registered in Anhui province might work and raise a family in Shanghai while retaining their original registration.</p>

<p>Three administrative distinctions are especially important:</p>

<p>Household registration (<em>huji</em>, 户籍): The system recording an individual's official household and registered locality; <em>hukou</em> commonly refers to the household registration or registered status.</p>

<p>Usual residence (<em>changzhudi</em>, 常住地): The place where someone ordinarily lives, which may differ from their registered locality.</p>

<p>Residence permit (<em>juzhuzheng</em>, 居住证): Documentation through which qualifying residents can access specified local services without necessarily transferring their household registration.</p>

<p>These classifications affect how local authorities administer access to public services. A residence permit can establish eligibility for particular benefits, but it does not guarantee admission to a preferred public school. Municipalities may apply additional conditions concerning parental employment, social-insurance contributions, residence duration and available school places.</p>

<h2>Can Children Without Local Hukou Attend Public School?</h2>

<p>Yes. Local <em>hukou</em> is not a universal requirement for receiving compulsory education where a child's parents live or work.</p>

<p>China's Compulsory Education Law guarantees nine years of education. Article 12 requires local governments to provide equal conditions for compulsory schooling to children whose parents or legal guardians work or reside outside their registered locality. The provision also leaves detailed implementation arrangements to provincial-level authorities (National People's Congress Standing Committee, 2018: Article 12).</p>

<p>The practical difficulty is therefore often not the absence of a legal entitlement, but the administrative process through which families exercise it.</p>

<p>National figures show substantial progress. According to China's 2025 Migrant Workers Monitoring Survey, 99.8 per cent of accompanying children of compulsory-school age were attending school. Among accompanying children of primary-school age, 97.8 per cent attended public or government-supported private schools; the equivalent figure for junior-secondary-age children was 95.8 per cent (National Bureau of Statistics of China, 2026).</p>

<p>These figures measure enrolment among children accompanying rural migrant workers. They do not describe every family living outside its registered locality, nor do they measure differences in school quality, placement or educational progression.</p>

<p>The distinction matters: attending school is not necessarily the same as accessing equivalent educational opportunities.</p>

<h2>Why Do School Admission Rules Differ Between Cities?</h2>

<p>National legislation establishes the right to compulsory education, but its delivery depends heavily on local administration. China's system assigns substantial responsibility to county- and district-level authorities, while provinces and the central government also contribute to financing and oversight (National People's Congress Standing Committee, 2018: Articles 7, 42; UNESCO, 2026).</p>

<p>Consequently, admissions procedures differ between municipalities and, in some cases, between districts within the same city.</p>

<p>Two examples from the 2026 admissions cycle illustrate how these arrangements work.</p>

<h2>Shanghai: Proving Residence and Employment</h2>

<p>Under rules published by the Shanghai Municipal Education Commission in March 2026, children accompanying residents registered outside Shanghai were required to hold a valid Shanghai residence permit or residence-registration certificate. At least one parent also needed a valid Shanghai residence permit and had to satisfy one of two employment-related conditions:</p>

<p>At least six months of qualifying Shanghai employee social-insurance contributions between 1 July 2025 and 30 June 2026, excluding retrospective contributions; or</p>

<p>Three consecutive years of registered flexible employment, measured up to 30 June 2026 (Shanghai Municipal Education Commission, 2026).</p>

<p>These conditions connect a child's eligibility for local compulsory-school enrolment to a parent's documented participation in the city's employment and registration systems.</p>

<p>Families must supply the relevant records, which district education authorities verify. Qualifying for admission, however, does not guarantee a particular school: districts arrange placements according to their published procedures and available capacity.</p>

<p>Shanghai thus illustrates how a child's educational entitlement can depend on administrative evidence concerning a parent's circumstances.</p>

<h2>Guangzhou: Allocating Places Through Points</h2>

<p>Guangzhou's Baiyun District operates a different mechanism. Its points-based admissions scheme, <em>jifen ruxue</em> (积分入学), allocates designated places in public and government-subsidised private schools to eligible children of non-local residents.</p>

<p>Under the district's 2025 rules, applicants must hold a qualifying Guangdong residence permit issued or endorsed in Guangzhou for at least one year. They must also meet at least one additional condition, including recognised stable residence in Baiyun, qualifying local business registration, current participation in a local social-insurance scheme, or a child's sixth-grade enrolment in a Baiyun primary school. Applicants must also have valid points recorded through Guangzhou's migrant-resident points-management system (Baiyun District Education Bureau and Human Resources and Social Security Bureau, 2025).</p>

<p>Eligible families can submit up to five school preferences. Places are allocated through a computerised system that combines applicants' points, preferences, and available school places. Where none of a family's preferred schools can offer a place, the district may arrange an alternative placement if the applicant has agreed to reassignment. Applicants who decline reassignment are not included in that additional allocation process (Baiyun District Education Bureau, 2026).</p>

<p>The scheme also requires families to apply again when a child moves from primary to junior secondary school. Receiving a primary-school place through the points system does not automatically secure a junior-secondary place under the same scheme (Baiyun District Education Bureau and Human Resources and Social Security Bureau, 2025).</p>

<p>Shanghai and Baiyun therefore illustrate two different administrative mechanisms. Shanghai's requirements establish an eligibility threshold linked to documentation and employment history. Baiyun's scheme additionally ranks eligible applicants for designated school places.</p>

<p>Neither scheme represents every possible admissions route within its municipality. Both nevertheless demonstrate how local authorities translate a national educational entitlement into specific administrative procedures.</p>

<p>Consider two migrant families living in the same urban district. Both have children approaching school age. One parent has uninterrupted social-insurance records and complete documentation; another changes employers frequently or works without a continuous formal employment record. Both children possess a legal entitlement to compulsory education, but the second family may face greater difficulty satisfying employment-based admission conditions.</p>

<p>The consequences can extend beyond the initial application. Placement arrangements may affect which school a child attends, whether it is publicly operated or government-supported, and how far the family must travel.</p>

<h2>Who Pays for Migrant Children's Education?</h2>

<p>Educational access is also a question of public finance.</p>

<p>China's education system is highly decentralised. County-level governments carry major responsibilities for administering and financing compulsory education, supported by central and provincial funding arrangements. Central transfers contribute to operating costs and help address differences in fiscal capacity between regions (UNESCO, 2026).</p>

<p>Population inflows can increase demand for classrooms, teachers and recurrent spending in receiving cities. Meeting that demand requires both sufficient physical capacity and a financing system that recognises where pupils actually live.</p>

<p>Documentation requirements and points-based allocation operate within this wider institutional setting. They establish eligibility and distribute designated places, but their existence alone does not prove that a particular municipality introduced them because central transfers were insufficient.</p>

<p>The underlying tension remains clear: national policy promises access to compulsory education, while much of the responsibility for delivering it falls on local governments managing changing resident populations.</p>

<p>The question is therefore not only who qualifies for a place, but whether education funding and school capacity keep pace with the children who need them.</p>

<h2>What Happens After Compulsory Education Ends?</h2>

<p>Compulsory education ends after junior secondary school, but a child's educational pathway continues.</p>

<p>At approximately fifteen, students seeking entry to senior secondary education generally take the <em>zhongkao</em> (中考), the senior-secondary entrance examination. Students who later pursue university admission generally take the <em>gaokao</em> (高考), administered through provincial-level examination and eligibility arrangements.</p>

<p>For children growing up outside their registered locality, these transitions can introduce additional conditions.</p>

<p>Shanghai's rules for the 2026 senior-secondary admissions cycle permitted several categories of non-local students to register. Under one major route, a student's parent needed a valid Shanghai residence permit meeting the prescribed points threshold, while the student also needed a valid residence permit and had to satisfy the relevant school-stage requirements (Shanghai Municipal Education Commission, 2025b).</p>

<p>Guangzhou's arrangements were different. Non-local students completing three years of junior-secondary schooling in Guangzhou could apply for admission to public senior high schools if they met the prescribed residence-documentation requirements. However, this did not confer access to every admissions mechanism: Guangzhou's separate quota-allocation route remained restricted to locally registered students and certain policy-protected categories (Guangzhou Admissions Examination Committee Office, 2026).</p>

<p>These differences can continue into university entrance.</p>

<p>Under one of Shanghai's 2026 university-admissions eligibility routes, a non-local applicant needed a parent whose residence permit met the prescribed points threshold, graduation from a Shanghai senior-secondary institution, and either participation in Shanghai's <em>zhongkao</em> or a parent who had held a Shanghai residence permit continuously for three years (Shanghai Municipal Education Commission, 2025a).</p>

<p>Admission to senior secondary school does not, by itself, establish eligibility for university entrance examinations in the same city.</p>

<p>The consequence is an uneven educational pathway. A child may spend nine years studying in a city without automatically qualifying for the same subsequent admissions opportunities as a locally registered classmate. Families unable to satisfy the relevant conditions may have to consider returning to their registered locality or pursuing another available educational route.</p>

<p>The point is not that all migrant children are required to leave their cities at fifteen. Rather, their ability to remain within a particular educational system can depend on administrative conditions that extend beyond academic performance.</p>

<h2>What Changed in 2026?</h2>

<p>On 18 May 2026, China's State Council issued its <em>Implementation Opinions on Providing Basic Public Services at the Place of Usual Residence</em> (<em>Guofa</em> [2026] No. 11).</p>

<p>The directive sets out a national policy direction towards providing basic public services where people ordinarily live, progressively reducing their dependence on local household registration (State Council, 2026).</p>

<p>For education, it instructs local governments to make better use of existing school capacity, create additional places where necessary and fund places in private schools when public provision cannot immediately meet demand. It also calls for better protection of school transfers and the progressive extension of local educational services into preschool and senior secondary education.</p>

<p>Crucially, the directive addresses financing alongside eligibility. It calls for education resources to respond more closely to population movements, supports the development of public senior high schools in receiving areas and proposes adjustments to fiscal transfers that take usual-resident populations into account.</p>

<p>The policy also addresses admissions restrictions directly. Where demand exceeds supply, local authorities may temporarily retain graduated allocation arrangements, including points systems based principally on residence and employment duration. However, the directive instructs them to reduce these distinctions over time and prohibits using educational qualifications, professional titles, or tax contributions as restrictive conditions for basic public services (State Council, 2026).</p>

<p>The chronology matters. Shanghai's March 2026 compulsory-school rules and Baiyun District's April 2026 guidance preceded the State Council directive. They illustrate the arrangements in place before the new national instructions, rather than demonstrating subsequent non-compliance.</p>

<p>The next test is implementation: whether future admissions cycles, including 2027, bring revised eligibility rules, sufficient school funding and additional places for children living outside their registered locality.</p>

<h2>The Distinction That Matters</h2>

<p><em>Hukou</em> continues to shape educational access because residence, household registration and eligibility for local public services have not become fully interchangeable.</p>

<p>China has achieved high compulsory-school enrolment among children accompanying rural migrant workers, and the May 2026 directive establishes a clear intention to reduce remaining administrative barriers. Yet admission to compulsory schooling, allocation to a particular school and progression into senior secondary education remain distinct institutional questions.</p>

<p>Whether reform produces more equal access will depend on how cities revise their rules, finance school provision and accommodate migrant children beyond the nine compulsory years.</p>
