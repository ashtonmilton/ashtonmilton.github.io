The home is conventionally imagined as a private sanctuary—a refuge of warmth, belonging and autonomy insulated from the market pressures of public life. Yet it is equally an economic and political institution in which labour is organised, resources are allocated and authority is exercised. Treating the household solely as an affective retreat obscures how deeply it is structured by wider norms surrounding gender, class, care and economic dependence.

This dual character produces a persistent analytical tension. A dwelling can generate genuine comfort and emotional attachment while simultaneously reproducing asymmetrical power relations. Resolving that tension requires looking beyond the house’s physical architecture to examine who performs the routine labour that sustains it, who controls household wealth and why the very privacy that protects some inhabitants can expose others to insecurity.

## Home, Belonging and Power

Shelley Mallett’s (2004) critical review of the literature on home demonstrates that the concept cannot be reduced to a physical structure. Home operates simultaneously as a material dwelling, a network of intimate relationships, an affective sense of belonging and an ongoing set of everyday practices.

That distinction is decisive for gender analysis. If a sense of home is actively produced rather than passively inhabited, sociology must ask whose labour manufactures that comfort. Cooking, cleaning, emotional maintenance and care coordination are demanding forms of work, even when they remain unpaid and are culturally coded as expressions of maternal or conjugal affection.

Historically, the male-breadwinner model associated women with unpaid domestic care and men with waged employment and household authority. While household arrangements vary significantly across class, ethnicity, sexuality and family form, gendered assumptions regarding who should care and who should earn continue to shape the domestic division of labour. Even as women’s paid labour-force participation has expanded across high-income economies, convergence in unpaid domestic work has remained strikingly incomplete (Gershuny and Sullivan, 2019).

## Cognitive Labour and Time Inequality

Domestic inequality is not merely a matter of counting hours spent on physical chores. As Allison Daminger (2019) demonstrates in her analysis of ‘cognitive labour’, household management entails anticipating needs, identifying options, making decisions and monitoring outcomes. One partner may handle routine tasks such as cooking or school pickups, while the other retains ongoing responsibility for planning and coordinating the household as a whole.

These asymmetries reverberate well beyond the front door. Disproportionate responsibility for unpaid care and mental load reduces time available for full-time employment, career progression, rest and civic participation. Over the life course, unequal domestic time translates directly into cumulative earnings gaps and lower pension wealth. An economic analysis focused exclusively on individual market wages therefore misses how paid labour capacity depends on unpaid reproductive work within the home.

## Economic Control Versus Budget Management

A parallel misconception arises around household money. Managing day-to-day expenditure—such as buying groceries, paying utility bills or budgeting for children’s clothing—is frequently mistaken for financial equality. In practice, administering routine consumption differs fundamentally from exercising strategic control over savings, property ownership, debt and major capital decisions.

Bina Agarwal’s (1997) framework on intra-household bargaining power clarifies why ownership and independent income matter so heavily inside the family. A person’s voice within domestic negotiations depends substantially on their ‘fallback position’—the material resources, housing security and social support they could command outside the household if the relationship dissolved. Economic dependence does not automatically imply coercion. However, when one partner lacks independent income, savings or secure tenure, their capacity to contest unfair divisions of labour or exit an untenable arrangement is severely constrained.

## The Limits of the Home as a Haven

The idealisation of domestic privacy also requires critical scrutiny. Privacy can shield families from intrusive external surveillance, yet it can equally insulate abuse from public accountability. As Evan Stark (2007) argues in his conceptualisation of ‘coercive control’, domestic abuse extends beyond episodic physical violence to encompass systematic financial restriction, social isolation and micro-regulation of everyday life within the private sphere.

Consequently, two people living under the same roof can inhabit starkly different social realities: for one, the home operates as a site of autonomy and comfort; for another, it functions as a space of surveillance and constraint. Treating the household as a single, harmonious unit with unified interests conceals these internal hierarchies.

## Gender, Displacement and the Work of Sustaining Home

The socially constructed nature of domestic roles becomes especially visible during war and forced displacement. Ilana Feldman’s (2006) research on memory and displacement in Gaza illustrates how home persists not merely as a lost physical address, but as an ongoing practice rebuilt through social ties, commemoration and daily routines.

Yuval-Davis (1997) examines how national projects assign women responsibilities for reproducing and sustaining a community. Read alongside Feldman’s account, that framework raises questions about how care and belonging are maintained during displacement. The two works do not, by themselves, establish a general account of changes in household gender relations across conflicts.

## Is the Home Inherently Unequal?

The home is not unequal by definition. Domestic labour, financial authority and care can be organised differently; the analytical task is to establish the conditions that support a more equal distribution. The literature discussed here does not justify a general empirical ranking of heterosexual and queer households.

Nonetheless, the domestic sphere remains a central site where gender inequality is reproduced precisely because it binds intimate affection to unpaid labour, asset ownership and unequal bargaining power. A rigorous sociology of the home must therefore look past sentiment to ask concrete structural questions: who performs the physical and cognitive work that makes belonging possible, who commands strategic financial authority and whose security is protected behind closed doors? Recognising that the home can function simultaneously as a refuge and a hierarchy offers a far stronger analytical foundation than either romanticising domestic life or dismissing it wholesale.
