When visitors describe a city as exceptionally clean, they frequently treat urban order as something almost self-generating. Streets appear free of litter, railway stations remain spotless and public facilities function without visible friction. The temptation—particularly in commentary on Japan—is to attribute that order directly to the inherent civic virtue of the population.

That culturalist explanation is appealing precisely because it is simple, yet it obscures how urban cleanliness is actually produced. Public space does not clean itself. What registers to the passer-by as effortless order is sustained by continuous physical labour, capital allocation, institutional enforcement and specific policy choices about who bears responsibility for waste. Cleanliness is therefore not merely a matter of private manners; it is a question of governance and distributive politics.

## The Invisibility of Maintenance Labour

The most decisive factor behind an orderly city is typically the least visible: routine maintenance labour. Pavements, concourses, drainage channels and public toilets remain usable because workers sweep, inspect, sanitise and repair them continuously.

As scholars of science and technology have long observed, infrastructure becomes most visible at the moment of breakdown (Star, 1999). A flooded underpass, an overflowing bin or a closed station lift immediately disrupts daily movement. Conversely, when maintenance regimes operate effectively, the labour sustaining them recedes into the background.

That invisibility carries political consequences. Describing a city as ‘naturally’ tidy can obscure the workforce that makes tidiness possible. Who employs cleaners, how contracts distribute responsibility and whether staff have adequate time and equipment are empirical questions. The polished surface cannot answer them.

## Capital Visibility Versus Maintenance Budgets

Urban authorities face persistent political trade-offs between building new assets and maintaining existing ones. Opening a new transit line, cultural venue or commercial district yields an immediate, tangible monument to political leadership. Routine street sweeping, gully clearing and sanitation upkeep rarely generate comparable political dividends, even though they shape daily living standards far more directly.

Unlike a one-off capital project, maintenance requires recurring expenditure, staffing and reliable procurement. Harris, Hodge and Phillips (2019) document pressures on English local government funding. Their report provides fiscal context, not sufficient evidence of a particular street-cleaning cut or its neighbourhood effects; those claims require service-level budgets and delivery records.

The distribution of maintenance deserves the same scrutiny as its overall funding. Comparing cleaning schedules, staffing and expenditure between neighbourhoods would help establish whether commercial and tourist areas receive different provision from residential districts. Visible differences alone do not explain the mechanism.

## How Policy Shapes ‘Cultural’ Norms

Social norms undeniably matter. When residents carry rubbish home, sort household waste rigorously and treat shared facilities with restraint, the operational burden on public sanitation shifts. In Japan, early school-based routines—such as students cleaning classrooms (o-sōji)—reinforce collective expectations around shared spaces (Tsuneyoshi, 2001).

Security policy also shapes disposal arrangements. Tokyo Metropolitan Government distinguishes two episodes: railway-station bins were removed after the 1995 sarin attack and later returned; wider removals began in 2004 following train bombings in Europe. The present scarcity of public bins should not be explained as the uninterrupted consequence of a single event in 1995.

Removing a bin does not remove the waste that people generate. Tokyo Metropolitan Government describes residents adapting by taking litter home. That account connects public-space management to household routines; it does not establish how much waste moves to convenience stores or what costs retail workers bear. Those would be questions for a more detailed investigation.

## Order, Exclusion and the Politics of Public Space

Visible cleanliness also functions as a powerful symbol of state competence. Municipal governments and business improvement districts have strong commercial incentives to project order, safety and predictability in high-visibility zones.

Yet a spotless commercial boulevard does not reflect the broader condition of a city’s housing stock, poverty rates or environmental health. Worse, regimes of urban order can easily slide from public maintenance into social exclusion. As urban geographers have documented in studies of ‘revanchist’ and sanitised city centres, aggressive street-cleaning ordinances, hostile architecture and privatised public-space policing often target unhoused residents, street vendors and informal livelihoods in the name of aesthetic hygiene (Mitchell, 2003).

Evaluating urban cleanliness therefore requires moving beyond surface impressions to ask concrete distributive questions: which districts receive recurring maintenance budgets, under what contracts are sanitation workers employed, how are disposal responsibilities divided between the state and citizens and who is excluded in order to preserve an image of effortless order?

## Seeing Cleanliness as a Political Choice

Clean streets are genuinely worth defending. Well-maintained public spaces confer dignity on everyday urban life, whereas neglected sanitation imposes immediate health and environmental costs on residents who cannot buy their way into private enclaves.

Treating cleanliness as evidence of a society’s innate character, however, prevents serious policy analysis. The more illuminating approach begins behind the tidy pavement and the spotless concourse. Urban order is an ongoing institutional accomplishment sustained through public revenue, regulatory choices and everyday labour. Recognising this reality turns an apparently mundane feature of city life into a sharper political-economy question: what does a society choose to maintain, through whose work and for whose benefit?
