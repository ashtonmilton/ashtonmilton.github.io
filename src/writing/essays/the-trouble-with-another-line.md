<p>At sixteen, you can legally consent to sex. At seventeen, you can drive. At eighteen, you can buy alcohol and vote in a UK general election. Meanwhile, Parliament is considering extending the vote to sixteen-year-olds, just as the government begins discussing new restrictions on whom they may legally have sex with.</p>

<p>I find that contradiction difficult to ignore.</p>

<p>On 24 November, the <a href="https://www.gov.uk/government/news/prime-minister-calls-time-on-abusers-hiding-in-plain-sight">government announced</a> that it would consider introducing a staggered age of consent. The intention is to protect teenagers from exploitation by significantly older adults. I understand the concern. A relationship involving a sixteen-year-old and someone decades older can raise serious questions about vulnerability, power and coercion. But recognising those risks does not automatically make another criminal prohibition the right response.</p>

<p>Under one <a href="https://closetheconsentgap.com/">campaign proposal</a>, a sixteen-year-old could legally have sex with a nineteen-year-old, but not a twenty-year-old. What precisely changes in that year? Age can indicate an imbalance of power, but it cannot establish whether somebody has been coerced, exploited or harmed. Nor does a relationship between people of similar ages guarantee equality.</p>

<p>We already distinguish consent from coercion in law, including specific protections where adults occupy positions of trust. If those protections are inadequate, surely the first task is to establish where and why they fail.</p>

<p>I am also uncomfortable with the tendency to treat criminalisation as an achievement in itself. A new offence might make previously lawful conduct prosecutable, but that does not necessarily mean it will prevent violence. It might increase recorded sexual offences without a corresponding increase in the underlying behaviour. Equally, it could expose exploitation that existing law struggles to address. Those possibilities deserve investigation rather than assumption.</p>

<p>More broadly, I think we need a coherent understanding of legal adulthood. Different age thresholds can be justified for different activities, but each should have a defensible purpose. We cannot continually appeal to young people's autonomy in one area and dismiss it in another without explaining the distinction.</p>

<p>Protecting young people from abuse is an important responsibility of government. So is respecting their agency.</p>

<p>Before drawing another line, ministers should explain exactly what the existing one fails to prevent, how the proposed change would address that failure, and what evidence would demonstrate that it worked.</p>

<p>Otherwise, we risk mistaking a change in the law for a change in people's lives.</p>
