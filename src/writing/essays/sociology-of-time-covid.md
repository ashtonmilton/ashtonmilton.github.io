The COVID-19 pandemic is routinely remembered as a moment when collective life came to an abrupt standstill. Schools closed, offices emptied, mobility narrowed and established schedules dissolved. Yet describing the pandemic as a universal pause conceals a fundamental sociological division: populations did not inhabit the same temporal reality.

The pandemic did not create a uniform surplus of time at home. Remote working could coincide with paid work, schooling and unpaid care in the same space. Meanwhile, essential workers continued working in person. Examining those different demands clarifies why a shared public-health crisis did not produce a shared experience of time.

## Time as a Stratified Social Institution

Clock time appears egalitarian because every day contains twenty-four hours. In practice, people socially organise and unequally govern time. As Eviatar Zerubavel (1981) demonstrated in his sociology of temporal order, schedules, calendars and institutional rhythms regulate social life and coordinate power. Employment contracts dictate when workers must be available; school timetables structure family routines; and state bureaucracies compel welfare or asylum claimants to endure prolonged delays.

Sociological inquiry therefore asks not simply how much time individuals possess, but how much sovereignty they exercise over it. Two people may live under the same lockdown decree while facing irreconcilable demands on their time. The pandemic did not suspend pre-existing class and gender hierarchies; it reorganised how those hierarchies registered in daily schedules.

## The Political Economy of Waiting

Khosravi’s (2021) discussion of waiting in border and dictatorial regimes offers a comparison, not an equivalence. The transferable insight concerns unequal control over delay: institutions can determine when others must wait and what uncertainty they bear. Asylum administration, however, involves legal status and coercive powers that cannot simply be equated with pandemic restrictions. The comparison helps formulate a question about temporal control rather than supplying evidence about lockdown itself.

During public-health lockdowns, the capacity to ‘wait out’ the crisis safely was mediated by occupational class and income security. Salaried white-collar workers with secure housing and broadband access could reorganise work and leisure inside the home. Conversely, workers in precarious, zero-hours or in-person service roles faced starker trade-offs: continuing to commute and work under heightened exposure, absorbing sudden income loss or waiting indefinitely for state income-support schemes to clear.

For essential workers across healthcare, food supply and municipal sanitation, the narrative of a general social pause was entirely fictitious. The apparent stillness of locked-down cities depended directly on the accelerated, continuous labour of workers whose time could not be paused.

## Domestic Compression and Time Poverty

Moving paid work, schooling and eldercare into the private dwelling at the same time also transformed household temporalities. Within confined living spaces, waged labour and unpaid domestic responsibilities competed directly across the same hours.

Elena Bardasi and Quentin Wodon’s (2010) economic formalisation of ‘time poverty’ provides a precise analytical tool here. Although their empirical study examined rural labour in Guinea rather than pandemic lockdowns, their central concept—that individuals become time-poor when combined paid and unpaid work leaves insufficient discretionary time for rest or choice—captures the exact mechanism triggered by school and nursery closures.

Cross-national pandemic data confirm how unevenly that burden fell. UN Women (2020) documented a sharp global rise in unpaid care and domestic labour during lockdowns, with women absorbing a disproportionate share of the additional hours. Similarly, real-time UK time-use surveys conducted during the 2020 lockdowns found that mothers were significantly more likely than fathers to interrupt paid work to manage childcare and homeschooling (Andrew et al., 2020). Time poverty immediately translated into economic vulnerability: workers carrying unpredictable care loads could not compete on equal terms with colleagues insulated from domestic disruption.

## Chrononormativity, Disability and Institutional Rhythms

Disabled people’s experiences require empirical evidence as well as a theory of time. In its survey covering February 2021, the Office for National Statistics reports that 40 per cent of disabled adults in Great Britain said the pandemic affected access to healthcare for conditions unrelated to coronavirus, compared with 19 per cent of non-disabled adults. The finding documents an unequal disruption to access; it does not identify every cause or describe every disabled person’s experience.

Freeman’s (2010) concept of chrononormativity provides a way to examine how institutional schedules presume particular bodies and capacities. Used alongside the ONS evidence, the concept directs attention to appointments, waiting and the organisation of support. It remains an interpretive framework, not empirical proof that every remote service or emergency timetable disadvantaged disabled people in the same way.

## The Unequal Horizon of the Future

Temporal inequality extends beyond managing a single day to shape how far ahead people can reasonably plan. As Pierre Bourdieu (2000) argued in his analysis of time and economic dispositions, the capacity to project oneself into the future presupposes a minimum threshold of material security in the present.

During the pandemic, households with liquid savings, secure tenure and salaried contracts could treat lockdowns as a temporary interlude. Households living paycheque to paycheque, facing eviction threats or experiencing disrupted education, confronted acute existential uncertainty. Epidemiological case counts and GDP figures routinely missed this lived divergence: two households experiencing the same national restriction timeline inhabited fundamentally different futures depending on their material buffers.

## What the Pandemic Revealed

COVID-19 made inequalities in temporal control more visible. Occupational arrangements shaped who could work remotely; divisions of care affected how paid work was interrupted; disability and access to support influenced how disruption was experienced. Those mechanisms require distinct evidence rather than a single account of a society placed on pause.

Incorporating a sociology of time into crisis analysis therefore sharpens how we evaluate social justice. Equitable public policy cannot be measured solely through aggregate income transfers or infection metrics; it must also be judged by who controls their hours, who is forced to wait in insecurity and whose indispensable labour keeps society functioning when clocks appear to stop.
