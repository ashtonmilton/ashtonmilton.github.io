In March 2026, Shanghai published its rules for admitting children to compulsory education. A child arriving with parents from another part of China could attend school in the city, provided the family met strict documentary and employment conditions: a valid Shanghai residence permit or residence-registration certificate for the child, alongside a residence permit and either six months of local employee social-insurance contributions or three consecutive years of registered flexible employment for at least one parent. Districts were required to arrange places through an online application system—a substantial advance over an era when internal migrants were routinely shut out of urban classrooms altogether (Shanghai Municipal Education Commission, 2026).

Yet those rules also expose the conditional nature of urban citizenship. A six-year-old’s claim to a local classroom still passes through a parent’s standing in the labour and registration apparatus. Before admitting the child, the municipality audits whether the household has made itself legible as a stable economic contributor.

Two months later, China’s State Council issued a national directive—Implementation Opinions on Providing Basic Public Services at the Place of Usual Residence (Document No. 11 [2026])—instructing local governments to decouple basic public services from local household registration. The directive spans migrant children’s schooling, social insurance, medical coverage, public housing, fiscal transfers and land quotas for schools and hospitals (State Council of the People’s Republic of China, 2026). Central authorities are effectively demanding that urban membership institutions catch up with urbanisation itself.

Whether they will depends on a political clash over money and authority. China built its cities around the labour of people whose official membership remained elsewhere. Turning residence into an unconditional claim on basic services requires dismantling the administrative tiers that allow wealthy municipalities to harvest migrant labour while rationing the social costs of reproducing it.

## Three Populations in One City

Chinese administrative language distinguishes between a city’s usual resident population (chángzhù rénkǒu) and its locally registered population (hùjí rénkǒu). A worker can live and pay taxes in Shanghai for a decade while retaining household registration (hukou) in another province. A residence permit (jūzhùzhèng) unlocks specific benefits without conferring permanent local hukou (Shanghai Municipal Government, 2023).

Living in a city and possessing local household registration remain distinct administrative positions. Non-local residents may hold residence permits and access services without acquiring permanent local hukou. That distinction matters because public-service eligibility and the funding of provision need not follow residence in the same way.

Nationally, the National Bureau of Statistics recorded 301.15 million migrant workers in 2025, including 180.06 million working outside their home localities and 130.92 million rural migrant workers residing in cities at year-end (National Bureau of Statistics of China, 2026). The same report records school enrolment of 99.8 per cent among accompanying children of compulsory-school age. The educational question is therefore not a general claim that migrant children cannot attend school: it concerns the type, location and quality of provision, family costs and progression to later stages. Solinger (1999) and Chan (2010) provide the wider account of economic participation without equivalent urban membership.

## What a Residence Permit Does—and Does Not Do

Urban registration reform has fractured along municipal lines. Smaller and medium-sized cities seeking workers and investment have largely opened local hukou, whereas Beijing and Shanghai maintain tight gateways designed to cap population and ration prestige infrastructure.

Beijing’s annual points-based settlement system (jīfēn luòhù) illustrates the distinction between residence and permanent registration: its July 2026 notice proposed 6,003 applicants for settlement through that channel (Beijing Municipal Human Resources and Social Security Bureau, 2026). That figure describes one route, not the total number of people gaining local registration. Shanghai’s residence-permit points system likewise distinguishes a permit from the additional entitlements associated with an eligible score (Shanghai Municipal Government, 2023). School admission must be examined through the applicable education rules, not inferred from settlement points alone.

The March Shanghai rules explicitly link baseline eligibility for accompanying children to parental documentation and employment records. Consider two children living nearby whose parents have different employment histories: the rules can place different administrative demands on those households. Establishing an unequal placement outcome, however, requires the relevant district allocation rules and admissions evidence rather than a hypothetical alone.

Basic educational entitlement should not depend on a parent’s credentials or employment status. Allocation within an oversubscribed system is a separate problem: proximity can reduce travel burdens, but it can also reproduce housing-market inequality when affluent households purchase access to desirable catchments. An equitable alternative would combine a guaranteed basic place with transparent oversubscription rules, scrutiny of catchment boundaries, transport support and funding for underserved districts. Unweighted lotteries may be appropriate for some contested places; no single allocation rule removes the need to examine school quality and residential segregation.

## What Implementation Must Establish

The State Council’s May 2026 directive is ambitious on paper. It mandates basic public services at the place of usual residence, instructs localities to expand public school capacity or purchase private places where shortfalls persist, abolishes hukou barriers to workplace social insurance and expands medical and social-assistance coverage for residence-permit holders. Crucially, while permitting temporary ‘graduated provision’ (tīdù gōngjǐ) based on residence and employment duration where demand exceeds supply, it orders localities to dismantle tiers over time and explicitly bars using educational attainment, professional titles or tax payments to ration basic services (State Council of the People’s Republic of China, 2026).

Chronology matters. Shanghai’s March admissions rules predate the May directive; they show the institutional starting point, not established non-compliance with a later instruction. The directive distinguishes prohibited criteria from transitional provision linked mainly to residence and employment duration. Assessment now requires subsequent local rules, admissions decisions and evidence of changes in access. Without those materials, a prediction of municipal resistance remains a hypothesis rather than an observed outcome.

## Who Pays When Residents Count?

Financing provides one possible mechanism through which reform may encounter difficulty. Where a municipality must expand classrooms or healthcare provision, extending entitlement creates spending needs. Whether transfers cover those costs, how quickly funds arrive and which level of government bears responsibility require evidence from the relevant budgets and rules. Those questions should not be collapsed into a presumption that every city has the same incentives or response.

The May directive links public-service provision more closely to usual residence and provides for adjustments to financial and land support. Those commitments may ease local constraints; a directive alone cannot establish how much capacity will be added or which households will benefit. Implementation needs to be examined at the level where services are funded and allocated.

The policy question is therefore practical: do resources arrive where additional residents need services and do they fund both new facilities and recurring provision? Shortfalls could reflect transfer design, population measurement, delivery delays or local priorities. Identifying which mechanism operates is necessary before recommending a particular funding formula or sanction.

## The Right to a City Should Not Be a Reward for Success

Points-based citizenship rests on a clear transactional logic: workers must prove economic utility, tax contribution and credentialed stability before claiming full membership in the city they help build. That logic inverts social protection’s purpose. Basic public services exist to insulate households—and above all children—from labour-market precarity, not to reward those who have already escaped it.

A defensible reform should protect basic service access without requiring households to surrender rural land-contract, homestead-use or collective-income rights. Scarce subsidised housing still requires transparent eligibility, prioritisation and financing, but rural household security should not become an unexamined price of urban membership. Residence duration and housing need can be assessed without treating an existing protected entitlement as disposable.

The test of China’s 2026 reforms is therefore concrete. Have Shanghai and Beijing stripped educational and tax points out of school admissions? Do central fiscal transfers actually follow migrant children into district education budgets? And can a parent in precarious work challenge an arbitrary administrative refusal? China’s cities have long depended on migrant labour as an engine of growth. A city becomes a genuine political community only when living within its borders—not holding the right pedigree or payroll record—establishes an equal claim to belong.
