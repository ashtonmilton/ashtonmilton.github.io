After living in Tokyo, I found myself returning to a question that initially seemed deceptively simple: why do major East Asian cities feel so much newer than European ones?

The explanation goes well beyond skyscrapers, cashless payments or dense railway networks. London possesses advanced infrastructure and vast capital reserves. Yet walking through Tokyo and London produces markedly different sensory impressions. The age of street surfaces, the spatial integration of railway stations, the turnover of surrounding building stock and the alignment between transport and commercial architecture all contribute to a sense that one urban environment is more recently fabricated than the other.

That impression is worth examining, but it is not a measure of social success. This essay concentrates on Tokyo and London; Seoul and Shanghai provide context rather than equivalent case studies. The question is how particular histories of construction and redevelopment become visible in everyday urban experience, not whether one region is inherently more modern than another.

## Cities Carry the History of When They Were Built

Urban space develops in historical layers and those layers reflect specific conjunctures of destruction, state-led reconstruction and capital investment.

Tokyo’s contemporary fabric bears the effects of the Great Kantō Earthquake of 1923, the destruction of 1945 and successive periods of rebuilding. Land readjustment is one institutional mechanism through which landowners and authorities reorganise plots and provide roads and utilities. Its effects are not uniformly co-ordinating: Sorensen (1999) finds that readjustment can limit sprawl within project boundaries whilst contributing to sprawl at the wider urban and regional scale. An orderly development project is not necessarily an orderly metropolitan development strategy.

Other East Asian cities reached their contemporary forms through markedly different political economies. Seoul’s rapid metropolitan expansion followed the devastation of the Korean War and state-directed developmentalism, relying heavily on large-scale apartment complexes (danji) and public-led land development corporations. Shanghai’s skyline, by contrast, reflects post-1978 market reforms, municipal land-lease financing and state-directed mega-projects such as Pudong’s transformation since 1990. Grouping Tokyo, Seoul and Shanghai under a single cultural or aesthetic label obscures the distinct state-market arrangements that built them.

European cities are no less products of rupture and renewal. London layers medieval street alignments, Victorian sewer and rail networks, post-war council estates and contemporary financial towers within walking distance. Cities such as Rotterdam and Warsaw underwent comprehensive post-war reconstruction. Europe is not a museum that merely preserves while East Asia rebuilds. Rather, the divergence lies in the historical timing, institutional organisation and regulatory speed of urban transformation.

## Development Comes in Different Waves

The historical era in which a city’s core networks expand shapes its physical form for generations. Cities that experienced explosive metropolitan growth during the second half of the twentieth century could embed modern engineering, high-capacity mass transit and integrated utilities directly into newly urbanising districts. Cities whose central arteries were laid down in the nineteenth century have had to retrofit contemporary demands onto inherited street geometries and fragmented property ownership.

Retrofitting is not inherently inferior. Inherited urban form often preserves human-scale walkability, civic squares and architectural continuity that rapid modernist expansion can erase. Yet it produces a visibly older built environment than concentrated waves of transit-oriented redevelopment.

The relationship between railways and property development offers a further institutional explanation. Calimente (2012) examines rail-integrated communities in Tokyo, where railway companies combine transport provision with development around stations. Stations can consequently function as commercial centres as well as interchanges. Comparable regeneration exists around European stations, including London’s King’s Cross; the relevant comparison concerns ownership, investment and planning arrangements rather than national character.

## The Limits of Visual Comparison

A station concourse or recently rebuilt commercial district makes redevelopment immediately visible. Housing security, public-service access and the distribution of redevelopment costs require different evidence. A visitor’s impression can generate a useful question without settling those wider judgements.

## Beyond Old and New

Some cities feel newer because their buildings and networks reflect different periods and institutions of urban transformation. The Tokyo–London comparison illustrates that possibility; it does not establish a uniform East Asia–Europe divide. Reconstruction, land assembly and railway-associated development help explain particular urban forms without making either city a model for an entire region.

Living in Tokyo sharpened my attention to how those arrangements register in everyday routines. A railway interchange, a rebuilt residential block or a commercial district surrounding a station reflects choices about land, finance and access.

The decisive question for urban policy is therefore not which city looks more futuristic. It is how different development trajectories distribute housing security, accessible public space and daily convenience—and whether the infrastructure that impresses an outside observer genuinely improves the lives of the people who sustain the city.
